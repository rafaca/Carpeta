Cargo Thinking

Straw soldiers. Bamboo control towers. Wooden landing strips. Post-WWII Melanesian communities built these to summon cargo planes back after Allied bases withdrew, performing the motions of exchange without the infrastructure that produced the goods. Five stories today find the same gap: care without its labor, taste without its social body, aspiration without substance.

01 / Brand Strategy

Symbol From Labor

When a manager pastes ChatGPT feedback into Slack, the problem isn't the quality. Jasmine Bina argues it's the contract, the implicit promise of partnership with someone who'd do the mutual labor of thinking. AI separates the symbol of care from its labor, and that separation feels like betrayal because it was a promise.

Bina maps the same violation across every register: the AI-first-person blog post breaks the reader's agreement; the partner who confides in a chatbot breaks the marriage contract. Every layer of social agreement — how to work, how to love, how to know what's real — is being rewritten at once.

"Symbols of care can be generated infinitely and for free in our new reality, and now the only thing that holds value is verifiable cost."Jasmine Bina

Her argument: in an economy where symbols of care can be generated infinitely and for free, what holds value is verifiable cost. Time, reputation, presence, risk. Things that carry consequence because they can't be undone. Bina calls this "the rebirth of the gifting economy." link

02 / Culture

Taste Needs a Witness

Emily Segal at Nemesis Memos coined a word for it — tasteslop. An AI-generated moodboard assembled from a Dieter Rams book, a skinny kettle, a Rimowa bag. A tech founder wearing Evan Kinori while raising SaaS capital. Tasteful objects deployed in service of slop.

K-HOLE co-founder Greg Fong supplies the working definition: taste requires a social body and social validation. AI can identify historical taste markers but cannot know when those markers have lost their meaning, or what would feel genuinely fresh next.

"Nobody can have taste unless somebody else can see it. The LLM is not a person."Greg Fong

Segal's argument: tech's obsession with taste reflects a specific anxiety. Cultural capital has become crucial for tech's distribution and relevance just as technology commoditizes. Tasteslop is the visible symptom, economic capital reaching for cultural capital, always arriving after the fact. link

03 / Influence

The Rimowa Effect

The lifestyle influencer whose entire content was the aesthetic apartment, the curated travel, the cute day, is getting comment-section notices: "Yeah, no one cares about your cute life anymore." Oren John at Hyper tracks this class of creator and says the backlash is now constant.

He calls the underlying dynamic the Rimowa Effect. When followers buy aspirational purchases they can't afford and the products don't deliver self-actualization, resentment redirects to the influence that sold them. The middle class, John argues, has been buying luxury out of range because influencers made it feel obligatory. Now that resentment is redirecting.

The market consequence: authentic voices with real resonance are winning by default. Brands paying for affinity rather than clicks are finding those relationships can change a business's trajectory. In 2026, John argues, influence matters more than ever — just not the aspirational kind. link

04 / Anthropology

The Bamboo Airplane

After World War II, when Allied bases withdrew from Melanesian islands, some communities built bamboo control towers, wooden airplanes, and straw soldiers to summon cargo planes back. They had the symbols. What they didn't have was the military, industrial, colonial infrastructure that had actually produced the goods.

Haena Chu at The Empty Cup traces how anthropologists read cargo cults not as magical thinking but as a response to an abrupt collapse of gift economies. In gift economies, goods carry moral obligation — giving creates cumulative social bonds. Money, by contrast, is impersonal: what anthropologist Marcel Mauss called a "moral vacuum."

The business world now uses "cargo thinking" as a warning against wanting results without the process. Chu's essay traces the inversion: these weren't irrational communities but ones performing the signs of exchange, hoping the symbols would summon the social relations that made exchange real. link

05 / Community

Pizza to Pews

100 people showed up the first week. By the third, 200 — driving from Long Island, taking the train from Boston. Anthony Gross, 22, and Kate DePetro organized it: dinner at The Pizza Box before the 6 p.m. Sunday mass at St. Joseph's in Greenwich Village.

Gross found the parish by asking ChatGPT where to find a young Catholic community. The post-mass discussion group at St. Joe's has grown from 25 to 200. Rev. Boniface Endorf: "People are looking for more than career and consumption. What does it mean to grow up? They're looking for guidance."

Casey Lewis covers this in After School alongside a New Yorker profile of a 12-year-old tween and the rise of multicultural teen slang — all pointing to a generation building rituals that cost something. Gross said: "Nobody wants to go to Mass alone. Better than going to a bar and spending $400." link
