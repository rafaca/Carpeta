01 / AI Economics

6,000x Cheaper

AI dropped 6,000x in cost over four years, Ruben Hassid writes — not by becoming perfect, but by becoming cheap enough to replace "good enough."

The Jevons Paradox says cheaper resources get used more, not less. Applied to intelligence: lower cost triggers more work, which means humans are expected to manage an output volume that didn't exist before. Junior knowledge work doesn't survive this math — the price gap closes before the quality gap. Hassid's better question: not "Will AI replace me?" but what changes when the thing you sell becomes nearly free. link

02 / AI & Work

Who Holds the Lens

At a California commencement, students booed Eric Schmidt discussing AI's impact on the arts — roughly 80% of college students, Iskander Smit notes, remain skeptical or critical of AI.

Smit's worry runs deeper. AI as exoskeleton extends your reach, finding connections you'd miss and synthesizing faster. But the threat comes when design decisions never ask who holds the lens. His example: XR training for delivery drivers could help them learn routes or could optimize every movement, turning agents into components — not by ideology but by accumulated choices nobody thought to examine. link

03 / Media Economics

Prof G's Margin Play

Scott Galloway moved his 400,000-subscriber newsletter to Substack in March — consolidating a media business that generates roughly $20 million a year in podcast revenue.

In two months, Prof G Media launched three new newsletters, prepares to debut a Friday portfolio roundup, and projects $1 million in Substack ARR by year-end. The move is about margin: the platforms before were built for enterprise advertisers, Galloway argues, not creators — where your margin is their opportunity. Substack, he told On, is purpose-built for the other side of that equation. link

04 / Strategy

The Structure of Feeling

Astrologers started appearing in Concept Bureau's comment sections — claiming they were tracking the same cultural signals as the futurists.

The convergence isn't random, Concept Bureau argues. Both are reaching for what Raymond Williams called the "structure of feeling" — the collective psyche where every cultural shift lives before it enters the market. The founders who built what the world needed before it knew it was ready weren't reading industry reports; they were tuned to the substrate. The inner precedes the outer, and that, Concept Bureau writes, is where the future actually comes from. link

05 / Brand Design

Context Beats Geometry

MaxiHealth's logo weaves M, A, and X into one continuous mark that shares geometry with Australia's ABC logo. BP&O argues this doesn't matter.

M/OTG's design is loopy, science-forward, reads as medical precision. Christopher Doyle & Co.'s Machine — a screen-printing company's mark — uses a cropped M that reads as a T-shirt draped between uprights, industrial against the fine-line trends in merch. BP&O's argument across both: context determines whether shared geometry is plagiarism or coincidence, and trusting people to tell the difference is part of the work. link
