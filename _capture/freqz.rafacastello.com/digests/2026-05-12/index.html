Earned, Not Bought

Ana Andjelic argues cultural fluency — fan obsession earned through meaning, not media spend — has become the real brand asset. Alongside it today: a fashion CEO on the industry's addiction to aspiration, a reality gap in how Americans perceive AI, Nintendo's new virtual stage for identity, and The Ordinary turning pricing logic into street theater.

01 / Brand Strategy

Fandom Beats the Funnel

Rhode became a $600M brand in two years; bootleg Gucci now outsells Gucci itself. Ana Andjelic's Hitmakers, Season 2, treats these as symptoms of the same shift: scale, price, and media spend no longer determine brand winners. Cultural fluency does — the ability to generate meaning, build worlds, and earn fan obsession that circulates through communities rather than transactions.

"People relate to these brands not in terms of price or category but as if they'd relate to music or art: with passion, identity and loyalty no media buy can manufacture."— Ana Andjelic, Sociology of Business

The brands doing it well — Rhode, Labubu, UGG, A24 — work less like consumer goods companies and more like creative studios with their own internal logic, aesthetic rules, and recurring narratives. link

02 / Fashion Industry

The Beautiful Addiction

Simon Whitehouse spent three decades in fashion's most influential rooms — JW Anderson CEO during the LVMH era, then Art Partner, Eco-Age, Reference Studios — before concluding the industry runs on a drug.

"We all worked as if this was going to happen, yes or yes. We would have done anything. And that's a drug."— Simon Whitehouse, 1 Granary

In Jorinde Croese's interview for 1 Granary, he speaks candidly about burnout, a suicide attempt, and the myth of glamour that traps young designers inside a system built for extraction. His sharpest critique: the industry sells aspiration to keep its own machinery running. link

03 / AI & Reality

We See AI As We Are

Americans now default to "this is fake" six times more often than "this is real" — but ZINE's YouGov survey of 1,000+ finds the confidence numbers reveal identity, not skill.

"Our perception of AI is shaped more by identity than reality itself."— ZINE

61% claim they can detect AI content. Yet older cohorts report more encounters and less confidence; men report more encounters and more confidence; conservatives default to "fake" and skip verification, liberals claim to verify within systems they already trust. Three demographic cuts produce three contradictory relationships between exposure and claimed ability.

ZINE's conclusion: the survey captures how culturally influenced self-reporting is, not actual detection accuracy. link

04 / Gaming & Fashion

The Island Is Open

Tomodachi Life: Living The Dream hit 3.8 million units in under a month, outselling Mario Kart World and ranking No. 42 among all Nintendo Switch titles ever — and no fashion brand has made a move.

Drawn & Distant traces the precedent: Animal Crossing became fashion's pandemic catwalk, with Valentino and Marc Jacobs both appearing. Tomodachi Life runs deeper custom clothing tools and looser restrictions, with 78.2 million TikTok posts already. The author argues the first-mover gap is wide open — the game's Mii-based identity performance is exactly where fashion brands should be competing. link

05 / Brand Campaign

The Markup Is the Message

Markup Marché, The Ordinary's new activation with Uncommon Creative Studio, presents a minimalist pop-up supermarket where a banana becomes an "All-Natural Magical Energy-Boosting Bar" and a coconut an "Exotic Thirst Defying Hydration Vessel."

The provocation is direct: the same language that justifies a $50 serum justifies a $3 piece of fruit. The pop-up, traveling to Toronto, London, Paris, Melbourne, São Paulo, and Mexico City, lands The Ordinary's brand argument — that inflated language manufactures perceived value — as an experience visitors shape, not observe. link
