ISSUE 23 · MAY 7, 2026 Frequencies

Out of Frame

Five pieces that converge on the same observation: the decisions that build durable value operate mostly out of sight. A 12-year-old builds a fashion brand before graduating elementary school; a novelist earns royalties from Connecticut on 13 million books while Meryl Streep gives interviews in Milan; a Japanese label turns a plasterer's trade into a complete brand universe. The Lakers run the same star-acquisition thesis seven times in 80 years; a home goods brand spends three days rehearsing a 60-second film and calls the process the point.

01 / Youth

The Unjaded Edge

North West is 12 years old and just dropped her debut EP. She hasn't graduated elementary school. James Davis of BOX EYE argues that what's happening now is children choosing industries, not industries choosing children — the floor to start something collapsed as Roblox, TikTok Shop, and Depop each stripped a layer off the barrier to entry.

In the US, 24% of late teens say they're already acting as entrepreneurs. The real driver, Davis writes, is risk reduction. When 89% of college graduates fear AI will replace entry-level jobs, building your own platform is contingency planning. link

02 / Design

The Plasterer's Universe

Refomed designer Yoshiki Ando named his jeans after his grandfather's trade, then built an entire cosmology around it. "Fresh Man" denotes the newbie plasterer who wears oversized pants to assert individuality; "Old Man" is the veteran who stopped caring.

The label does denim, hoodies, and zip jackets, all reworked with specific details — a kinchaku pocket that converts to a pouch, a zipper pull shaped like a leather key necklace. It also makes fragrances and publishes an infrequent magazine, Distill. Ando trained at undersung Japanese label Name., and it shows: Refomed has the worldbuilding of a much older brand. link

03 / Visibility

22,000 Followers

Lauren Weisberger wrote The Devil Wears Prada from a real-life ten-month stint as Anna Wintour's assistant. The book sold 13 million copies across 40 languages, became a film, and is now a West End musical with Elton John. Twenty-two thousand Instagram followers.

Weisberger lives in Connecticut, giving no interviews while Anne Hathaway walks red carpets in New York. Manual de Moda reads this as three distinct business models: Hathaway trades on permanent visibility; Streep on accumulated authority; Weisberger on royalties from a single story she turned into an asset and let compound for twenty years. link

04 / Franchise

Seven Stars

$16 million in 1979. $10 billion in October 2025. The Buss family compounded the Los Angeles Lakers 625 times over 46 years, beating the S&P 500 by a factor of five.

John Brewton traces the franchise's formula to seven identical bets over eight decades: acquire one transcendent star, then build everything else to protect that asset. Mikan, then Chamberlain, then Magic, then Shaq and Kobe in the same calendar week, then Gasol, then LeBron, then Luka Dončić — 25 years old, All-NBA First Team, who had not requested a trade. link

05 / Brand

Sixty Seconds

When AI compresses the cost of creative production, Lulu & Georgia spent days choreographing ballet dancers around furnishings for a 60-second film. VP Jenna Kincaid, 13 years at the brand, says the team pulled references from fashion, food, music, and theater, letting them mix into the details of interior design.

"We see a growing number of steps being taken to turn art into something 'easy' and instantaneous — with little regard for process or personhood."Jenna Kincaid, Lulu and Georgia

"There's an Art to It" films the space coming together, showing process rather than a finished room. Kincaid argues that as AI turns content creation instantaneous, the brand's edge is in how much human consideration goes into each decision. link
