§ 01 Taste as a Moat

When Natural Dye Becomes a Uniform

Ana Andjelic’s latest Sociology of Business arrives timed to Rick Rubin’s Rock and Roll Hall of Fame induction this November — but uses it as the springboard for something sharper. Taste is everywhere on Substack. So is The Devil Wears Prada 2. The movie about the foremost taste-making industry in the world almost certainly measured its promotional campaign in impressions, reach, and views. Andjelic’s point: impressions are evidence that people scrolled past you. They are not cultural relevance.

“Impressions, reach, and views are not cultural relevance. They are evidence that people scrolled past you.” — Ana Andjelic, The Sociology of Business

The free portion frames the collision between attention and taste as played out in real time through the film’s global rollout — celebrity cameos, Vanessa Friedman, method red carpet dressing, the popcorn purse, brand activations from Dior. Behind the paywall: the analysis of what different implications of attention versus taste mean for brand strategy.

Big Pants Waste Precious Fabric picks up this thread from the menswear end with a provocation: are natty-dye guys the new Nolita Dirtbags? The Evan Kinori discourse online has been surprisingly meaty — one poster’s exhaustive takedown argues that Kinori, the concept, offers “moral cover for wealthy people to spend exorbitant amounts on clothes whilst feeling principled doing so.” Big Pants’s sharper point: natty-dye guys are surface-level shoppers who graduated to Kinori through menswear osmosis. Blind kakishibu worship is Pitchfork scores for clothes. The fabrics are fine. The problem is when technique replaces personality.

PostCulture extends the sensory thesis beyond the visual. Florals have always been central to brand events, but lately they’re stepping into centre stage — less background, more focal point. Sculptural, excessive, hyper-considered. If last week’s food-as-status-signal was about consumption, flowers are about composition. And scent itself is becoming the new IYKYK access code: perfume stacking, sense-based marketing as an antidote to the screen.

A parallel current surfaces in Dot Dot Dot’s dispatch from Milan Design Week, where Product Anatomy went live with Stone Island and Lyst. The event happened in a drained swimming pool inside Capsule Plaza. A 4×8-metre LED ceiling. Seating upholstered in NO SEASONS fabric. Archive folders passed hand to hand through the audience. Alberto Ghidotti explained the jacket’s construction “uncomfortably close, which was the point.” That’s taste as proximity, not reach. Part 2 is confirmed for October in London.

And on the craft end: B Pando reviews Sooki sesame oil by The Collected Works under the subject line “Like, so retro, but so the future.” The brand centers on a mascot named Sooki Boy — rechargeable sesame oil arms, a traditional Bokgeon cloth hat, rocket feet — rendered with custom ink-ish letterforms and a texture palette that begs to be touched. The typography uses Gatwick by Pangram Pangram. It could go too cute. It doesn’t. The colour palette (vibrant red, dark blue, off-white) keeps it classy throughout.

§ 03 Every Abstraction Leaks

Call the Plumber

a16z publishes the essay of the week. The premise: mainstream doomerism is about what will fall apart, but the subtler, valid version is a recognition that many of the world’s load-bearing abstractions are going to leak a lot in the coming years. Abstractions, in this sense, are reliable interfaces built out of less-than-reliable parts. TCP on top of IP. Global commodity markets on top of uncertain contracts. And the critical thing: when people see the details underneath, they try to grab the controls themselves.

“The world’s abstractions will leak; a lot. So get to know some plumbers.” — a16z

The essay traces four case studies. Shopify checkout: a form that looks simple but negotiates warehouse locations, estimated tax, credit card processing, and sovereign privacy mandates behind the curtain. Paper checks and DOGE: Treasury tried to kill paper checks ($2 each to process), then quietly paid a third party to keep issuing them when edge cases — unbanked individuals, estates, international taxpayers — proved the abstraction was load-bearing. Hormuz: the global energy market has held up remarkably well with the Strait shut for eight weeks, but everyone is now aware of “paper barrels versus physical barrels,” and countries are cutting bilateral deals that bypass the old multilateral system. The UAE just left OPEC. The internet: the original public internet is a thing of the past. It’s more accurate to imagine many private internets that seamlessly hand off and interact.

The Canadian PM Mark Carney, speaking at Davos in January, closed the door on the old order: “There is no ‘single global anything’ anymore; there are only arrangements of mutual interest.” The great 20th-century abstractions — free trade, global commodity markets, the dollar system, Pax Americana — were so smooth that outsiders could treat them as one solid thing. They held for a very long time. But just as the author has had to find alternate arrangements for paying the IRS from abroad, don’t be surprised to see sovereign actors harden their defenses.

One possible beneficiary over the long run: what Byrne Hobart calls “deeply horizontal companies” — an odd collection of tech giants that address the need for end-to-end defenses. The message for operators: study eras where our modern abstractions hadn’t yet formed, like early maritime insurance. The world is too motivated and too skilled to collapse. But it will leak.

§ 04 The Rockefeller Playbook

Three Decisions, $380 Billion

The Operating by John Brewton publishes a definitive operational history of Anthropic. The company now generates $30 billion in annualized revenue, carries a $380 billion valuation, and has received unsolicited offers at $800B. Claude Code went from zero to $2.5 billion in annualized billings in nine months. Three consecutive years of roughly 10x annual revenue growth at this scale is unverified in the enterprise technology record.

Businesses spending $1M+/year on Claude

Brewton’s framework: three decisions made Anthropic. A method decision: safety as the product, not a constraint. Constitutional AI gave enterprise sales verifiable claims that pharmaceutical, financial, and public-sector buyers required before deploying AI in consequential workflows. A constraint decision: when Anthropic had a model capable of hundreds of millions in annual revenue (Claude Mythos), it restricted it to forty cybersecurity partners. Trust became the moat. A vertical integration decision: the model is the refinery. Claude.ai is retail distribution. MCP is the pipeline network. Claude Code is the developer’s output. Cowork is the business user’s output. Claude Design is the designer’s output. The Standard Oil pattern compressed.

“Every business that sells a tool gets commoditized. Every business that sells an outcome gets to charge for the value of the outcome.” — John Brewton, The Operating

The historical parallels run deep: the Traitorous Eight at Fairchild (method over hierarchy, 1957), Andy Grove’s memory-to-microprocessor pivot at Intel (trust as the moat, 1985), and Rockefeller’s climb up the Standard Oil stack (vertical integration, 1870–1890). The Cowork release wiped $285 billion in market cap in 24 hours because the market understood Anthropic had completed the Rockefeller move on the SaaS layer.

Houck extends this into the startup layer with “7 Attributes of AI-Native Startups.” The core: your moat is your data flywheel. Make your company queryable — every process machine-readable, public channels over DMs. Track intelligence-per-dollar, not just revenue per employee. The new org chart has three roles: Builder-Operator, DRI, and Founder. Human middleware that routes information is no longer a job for people.

§ 05 The Hot Mess Problem

Spending Millions to Be Completely Irrelevant

The Slow AI dissects a new Anthropic paper at ICLR 2026: “The Hot Mess of AI.” The finding upends the core assumption behind every AI deployment: the smarter the model, the more unpredictable its failures on the tasks that matter. The paper splits error into bias (systematic, auditable) and variance (random, invisible). On hard tasks, bigger models become less reliable. Understanding and execution come apart as models grow. You cannot audit randomness. You cannot train a human to spot errors that don’t follow a pattern.

Garbage Day delivers the cultural analogue of that hot mess. The industrial clipping complex on Kick has turned short-form video into a pay-to-play wasteland. Bloomberg reports that Clipping, the company behind the Kick clip flood, openly admits to gaming every algorithm. Kick streamer N3on spends over $1 million a month on clipping. The profoundly unsettling question: how can someone be spending over $12 million a year to promote themselves and still be a complete cultural nonentity?

“What was once a place where you could directly connect with audiences is now a place where you have to spend millions of dollars to get anyone to see your stuff. And you don’t even get to be actually famous.” — Ryan Broderick, Garbage Day

At the bottom of the algorithm across every platform: AI slop, old memes, subtitled movie clips, softcore porn, gambling ads, celebrity podcast clips, and livestreamers. The parallel to the AI paper is structural: both describe systems where the legibility of quality has broken down. In AI, the confident wrong answer looks identical to the right one. In short-form video, the million-dollar promotion looks identical to organic virality. Neither abstraction holds.

From Sidebar: another leaky abstraction — accessibility. “The Accessibility Problem Isn’t Design. It’s Engineering.” Full-stack hiring, AI-generated code with no context, and a compliance-first mentality have combined to create a crisis hiding in plain sight. Also featured: “The Moat or the Commons” — American capital financed AI on the assumption it would be the next great monopoly. What if it’s the commons instead?

§ 06 World & Markets

Earnings Quad-Kill and the Voting Rights Gutting

Big Tech’s accountability moment arrives after the bell. Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta all report Q1 2026 earnings in the same 48-hour window. Together, they’ve committed close to $650 billion in AI infrastructure spending for 2026. Microsoft alone spent $37.5B last quarter. The question: has any of it translated into worthwhile revenue? Meta expects $55.5B in revenue and $6.71 EPS. Amazon projects 14% revenue growth to $188B. This week is AI capex’s first real accountability test.

The Supreme Court’s Wednesday decision all but dismantled the landmark law that came out of the Civil Rights Movement. A generational rollback.

Emphasized defending Ukraine, sidestepped Epstein survivors’ direct request. Trump rejects Iran’s proposal to reopen Hormuz without nuclear guarantees.

Energy

Oil Surges, UAE Exits OPEC

WTI at $99.93/bbl (+3%), Brent at $111.26. The UAE left OPEC. Strait of Hormuz shut for 8 weeks. Brazil and India cut a bilateral deal.

Markets

SpaceX IPO Rule Change

Nasdaq’s new “Fast Entry” rule lets SpaceX enter the Nasdaq-100 in 15 days. A 3x free-float multiplier inflates index weight. Passive investors at risk.

El Ingeniero de Wall Street details the SpaceX IPO mechanics: SpaceX plans to float only ~4.3% of its shares at a $1.75T valuation. Nasdaq’s new rule multiplies the free float by 3x to calculate index weight. Index funds will be forced to buy at inflated prices, then forced to sell when the lock-up expires and the multiplier deactivates — a mechanical loss of ~54% on the SpaceX position. Michael Burry and several independent analysts have flagged this as a structural risk to passive investors. Goldman Sachs, the IPO’s placement bank, minimizes it publicly.

Also: S&P 500 down 0.49% to 7,138.80, Nasdaq down 0.9%. FCC ordered a review of ABC licenses after Trump demanded Kimmel be fired. Former FBI Director Comey indicted a second time. South Korea’s Yoon sentenced to 7 years. The Trump administration fired all members of the National Science Board.
