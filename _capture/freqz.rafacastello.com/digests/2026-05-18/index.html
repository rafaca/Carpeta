01 / Music / Commerce

The Posthumous Dividend

The Michael Jackson biopic earned $700 million in four weeks — second-biggest music biopic in history, past Elvis and closing on Bohemian Rhapsody. His back catalog returned to the charts in a spillover effect.

Gioia argues this is what the industry calculated. Hollywood wagered $200 million on an artist whose private life made the project radioactive; mainstream audiences answered with the biggest opening weekend the biopic genre has seen. Cancel culture, Gioia writes, has no economic teeth: it is social media noise. The money goes where the audience wants it. link

02 / Brand / Culture

The Obsession Premium

Labubu is a plastic toy and a $1 billion cultural phenomenon. A24 audiences will see anything with that logo. Rhode built $600 million in beauty on aesthetic trust alone.

Tasty Magazine traces the pattern: these brands created conditions for obsession, then got out of the way. The attention economy rewards speed and shock; the obsession economy rewards depth and specificity — the thing that keeps revealing more the longer you engage with it, that you find yourself recommending to others who will understand why it matters. link

03 / AI / Mastery

The Grammar of Craft

Down, down-forward, forward, punch: when David Hoang's eight-year-old self pulled off a fireball in Street Fighter II, he understood that special moves share a grammar across fighting games.

"The move list in the manual had been teaching me the wrong lesson; I thought I was memorizing facts about Ryu. I was actually learning a language."David Hoang, Proof of Concept

Hoang maps the same grammar onto every creative craft. Keyboard shortcuts chunk six menu trips into one fluid motion; AI prompts are the newest combos to master. The cognitive science calls this chunking: a practiced sequence migrates out of working memory and runs on its own, freeing your attention for the thing the hand is in service of. link

04 / AI / Publishing

The Delegation Limit

Jamie Thingelstad has published the Weekly Thing every weekend for nine years. For this year's anniversary issue, he introduced a four-agent support team in Discord: Eddy reviews every draft, Linky does link recon and flags weak picks, Marky handles promotion, and Patty runs the supporting membership program.

All four agents work from the full nine-year archive. Thingelstad drew a hard line on what they don't do: none of them write content. The agents take on the structural work — assembling drafts, vetting links, managing community — that would otherwise compress the time available for the writing itself. link

05 / Design / Media

The Form Still Holds

The New York Times Magazine launched its first redesign in 11 years, reaching 13 million subscribers across print, mobile, audio, video, and social. Design director Ben Grandgenett built the redesign around a single question: what should a magazine be now?

Grandgenett's answer is structural. The redesign creates space for recurring storytelling forms that can move between the Magazine and the broader Times report, connecting channels that didn't exist in 2015. New custom typefaces — reworked versions of Cheltenham and Franklin Gothic, developed with A2-TYPE — extend the visual identity from print to digital. link
