What Speed Can’t Touch

Milan’s Salone proved that luxury still grows where it can’t be accelerated—forty fashion houses, stone groceries, and a £1.4B bet on velvet-rope access. Meanwhile, AI devours the middle of every org chart: Snap discloses 65% AI-generated code, Google claims 75%, and entry-level dev postings have collapsed 67%. Anthropic hits $30B ARR in five years while Google invests $40B in the competitor eating its enterprise market alive. The pattern: high-touch where it can’t be sped up, hyper-speed everywhere else.

§ 01 Luxury & Design

Analog Supremacy

Salone del Mobile 2026 drew 316,342 visitors from 167 countries, up 4.5% year-over-year. But the real story is who showed up to play: more than forty fashion houses staged Fuorisalone events, giving fashion 56% share-of-voice on social during Design Week against 30% for design itself. Gucci, Louis Vuitton, Hermès, Prada, Miu Miu, Bottega Veneta, Loro Piana, with Jil Sander debuting. The level of creativity on the ground was extraordinary—and possibly approaching peak.

The pattern underneath: the categories of luxury still growing are built on physical access, in-person craft, and experiences that resist acceleration. DIAFA, an Abu Dhabi luxury-hospitality vehicle controlled by Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed, paid £1.4B for Richard Caring’s empire (Annabel’s, Scott’s, Sexy Fish, The Ivy Collection) at a fifteen-times multiple. It’s their third luxury F&B acquisition in seven months. The playbook: roll up culturally-defining venues, run them as brands rather than restaurants, sell membership and access as the product, then export.

Separately, Black Tomato’s “Get Lost” program—$10K to $30K to be dropped into a polar environment and walk yourself out with a satellite tracker watching from a distance—represents the same thesis. The survival-training market is projected to hit $6.52B by 2030. The people writing the biggest checks are not preppers. They’re founders with recent liquidity events.

§ 02 AI & Labor

The Middle Gets Eaten

On April 15, Snap fired 1,000 engineers and disclosed that 65% of new code is now AI-generated. A week later, Sundar Pichai posted that 75% of new code at Google is AI-generated, up from 25% in late 2024. The Leverage ran a fascinating analysis: engineering job postings hit 67,000 globally in March 2026—the highest in years—while entry-level developer postings dropped 67% between 2023 and 2024, and employment for software developers aged 22–25 fell roughly 20% from its late-2022 peak.

The theory: AI is not compressing code (prompts are roughly the same length as the Python they produce). It is transferring capabilities up and down the org chart, hollowing out the middle. Every knowledge workflow has a brief writer, a translator, a producer, a reviewer, and a judge. When an abstraction layer arrives, the brief writer gets empowered (they ship directly through prompts), the translator and producer get eliminated (the AI absorbs them), and the reviewer and judge get promoted (their taste becomes the constraint on output quality).

The translator and producer roles are exactly what we used to call “junior” and “mid-level.” The reviewer and judge roles are exactly what we call “senior” and “specialist.”—The Leverage

Sidebar led with a Linear essay titled “Output isn’t design”—challenging the assumption behind AI design tools that design is the act of producing. Alongside it, a Frontend Masters piece arguing that AI-generated UI is inaccessible by default. The a16z Design Engineer Fellowship, announced today, seeks AI-native design leaders who have absorbed engineering into their craft. The signal is consistent: the craft layer survives; the production layer doesn’t.

§ 03 Business & Technology

The Anthropic Machine

John Brewton’s Operating Stories published the most comprehensive Anthropic analysis yet, mapping the company’s trajectory to three historical parallels: the Fairchild Traitorous Eight, Andy Grove’s Intel pivot, and Rockefeller’s Standard Oil stack climb. The numbers are staggering: $30 billion in annualized revenue, $380 billion valuation, Claude Code from zero to $2.5 billion in billings in nine months. Three consecutive years of roughly 10x annual revenue growth at enterprise scale is unverified in the technology record.

The operating thesis distills to three decisions. First, safety as the product (Constitutional AI made procurement possible for regulated buyers). Second, building from the model up rather than the chatbot down (each release captures more of the value that used to flow to the application layer). Third, governance preceding revenue every time (Claude Mythos restricted to forty cybersecurity partners rather than sold broadly).

Meanwhile, App Economy Insights framed the Google paradox: Alphabet plans to invest up to $40 billion in Anthropic ($10B upfront, $30B tied to milestones, plus 5 gigawatts of computing capacity) at a $350B valuation—while secondary markets price Anthropic at $1 trillion this week. Google Cloud revenue hit $20B in Q1 FY26, up 63% year-over-year. The infrastructure layer matters more than model loyalty.

The Cowork launch in February wiped $285 billion in market capitalization from the ten largest US enterprise software companies in 24 hours. The market understood that Anthropic had completed the Rockefeller move on the SaaS layer: every enterprise software category charging per-seat for a specific UI on top of specific data had just been informed that the UI was optional and the data was accessible through a new control layer.

§ 04 Branding & Strategy

Culture Without the Work

The Art of Branding published a sharp playbook on what “cultural brand” actually means—and why the word “community” has become structurally empty. The distinction that matters: a culturally relevant brand understands a cultural context and communicates fluently within it. A culture-led brand originates from inside one. The first can lose relevance through a misstep. The second has structural durability because the credibility is not communicative—it’s baked into the product, the team, the relationships.

Fenty Beauty remains the clearest illustration. The gap it identified was not a representation gap in advertising. It was a product gap: an entire spectrum of skin tones the industry had systematically excluded from its core offering. The structural decision—design for the full range from the beginning—produced both the cultural resonance and the commercial outcome. Competitors who had operated for decades without a full shade range discovered within months that they needed one.

Manual de Moda’s Tamara complemented this from the personal angle: the day she left Givenchy, she lost everything structural (title, office, LVMH discount, org-chart legitimacy) and recovered everything personal (freedom, immediate utility, unmediated credibility). Her point: while you depend on the name, the title, or the structure, your growth always has a ceiling you don’t control. The day you sustain yourself with your own judgment, the brand is you.

Claire’s Chief Brand Officer on their comeback appeared in People, Brands & Things—another iconic American brand rebuilding structural credibility rather than just running nostalgia campaigns.

§ 05 Experience & Senses

Senses as Moat

Post Culture explored how scent is displacing taste as the status signal for brand events. Floral arrangements have moved from background to focal point—sculptural, excessive, hyper-considered. If food was about consumption, flowers are about composition. Perfume has become the IYKYK access token; scent stacking is the new layering game. The argument: sense-based marketing functions as an antidote to AI-generated content because it is irreducibly physical.

This connects to INTAKE’s through-line about the categories of luxury still growing. The pattern is consistent: Black Tomato sells you who you are when you come back. Annabel’s sells access. Salone sells presence. None of these can be compressed, prompted, or scaled without destroying what makes them valuable. In a world where AI compresses every knowledge-work deliverable, the irreducible thing is being there.

Why Is This Interesting ran “The Performative Fitness Edition”—on Strava, streak tracking, and the validation trap. When the performance of an activity replaces the activity itself, the thing loses its power. The analog moat only works if the analog remains genuine.

Spencer Chang’s Tiny Internets explored the same tension from the digital side: “The Internet has no benches.” His argument for building free public digital infrastructure designed for enjoyment—lingering, not optimizing—maps directly onto the physical-presence thesis.

§ 06 World & Markets

World & Markets

The S&P 500 rose 1.02% and the Nasdaq hit a new all-time high, up 0.89%. The Dow added 790 points. Investors reacted to upbeat earnings from Caterpillar (+10%), Eli Lilly (+7% on raised guidance to $82–85B), and Qualcomm (+16%). Alphabet beat estimates with $110B revenue (+22% YoY), cloud at $20B (+63%). Meta fell 9% after hiking CapEx guidance to $125–145B for AI infrastructure.

GDP grew at a 2% annualized pace in Q1, up from 0.5% in Q4 2025 but below the 2.2% estimate. Oil whipsawed: Brent surged to a wartime high of $126/barrel before falling back to $114 on US-Iran tension dynamics. California gasoline hit $6 per gallon, a 30% increase since the US-Israel campaign against Iran began in late February. Day 61 of the conflict; Defense Secretary Hegseth at a congressional hearing called lawmakers who criticize the effort “the biggest adversary.”

Domestically: the House approved a three-year extension of FISA Section 702 surveillance; the Supreme Court ruled Louisiana must redraw a congressional map designed to create a second majority-Black district; and the conservative majority appeared to side with the administration’s authority to revoke temporary protected status for Haitian and Syrian immigrants.

In tech beyond AI: Google launched Gemini 3.1 Ultra with a 2-million token context window across text, image, audio, and video. OpenAI reportedly at $25B ARR and taking early steps toward a public listing. Apple’s foldable iPhone expected fall 2026 (7.8-inch inner display, 5.5-inch outer).
