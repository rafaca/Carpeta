Infrastructure

The Subsidy Cliff: AI's Pricing Party Is Over

Three separate newsletters converged on the same story this week: the economic foundations of AI services are cracking. The era of flat-rate subscriptions that let users burn $8–$13 worth of compute for every $1 of subscription revenue is coming to an abrupt end.

Benedict Evans framed Anthropic's capacity problems through the lens of mobile data in the late 2000s — when the iPhone's flat-rate plan swamped AT&T's network. His prediction: "The natural end-state of all these situations is a segmented range of flat-rate bundles." Anthropic, he notes, has invested least in capacity of all the big labs, and now agentic coding has found massive product-market fit, making that a real problem.

Anthropic should stop taking on new customers until it works out its capacity issues. Ed Zitron, Where's Your Ed At

Ed Zitron went much further in his "Four Horsemen of the AIpocalypse" edition, documenting Anthropic's Claude chatbot at 98.79% uptime — which translates to nearly four and a half days of downtime per year. Enterprise clients are reportedly switching to OpenAI. The newly launched Opus 4.7 is drawing complaints of worse performance and higher token burn, with 2,300 upvotes on a Reddit post titled "Claude Opus 4.7 is a serious regression, not an upgrade."

The real kicker: Microsoft is suspending new GitHub Copilot signups and preparing to move customers to token-based billing. Per Zitron's reporting, Copilot's week-over-week costs have nearly doubled since January. Anthropic has already shifted enterprise customers to API-rate pricing. A Goldman Sachs report reveals some companies are spending 10% of engineering headcount cost on AI tokens — and the trajectory points to 100% within quarters.

Meanwhile, Uber blew through its entire annual AI budget a few months into 2026, with 11% of backend code updates now written by AI agents. The OP Letter's links section corroborated the trend, citing reports on AI compute scarcity and rising enterprise costs from multiple sources.

Zitron's call: by end of 2026, a majority of AI services will move to token-based billing. The subsidy era is over; the question is whether the demand survives the sticker shock.

§ 02

Leadership

Apple After Cook: The Ternus Era Begins

The biggest corporate succession story of the year dropped quietly on a Sunday evening. Tim Cook will become Executive Chairman effective September 1, 2026, with John Ternus — SVP of Hardware Engineering — stepping in as Apple's eighth CEO.

Public Announcement linked to Gruber's Daring Fireball analysis, which characterized the transition as "notably drama-free" and reflective of Cook's management style: orderly, planned, methodical. Ternus is approximately 50 — matching Cook's age when he took the role. Johny Srouji was simultaneously named Chief Hardware Officer.

If the Steve Jobs era was defined by technological innovation, the Tim Cook period was one of exceptional financial growth. The New York Times

The signal: continuity over revolution. Ternus's hardware background suggests emphasis on product design and engineering excellence — a potential counterweight to the software-and-services narrative that defined Cook's later years. Meanwhile, the Siri team is reportedly being sent on AI "bootcamps," suggesting Apple knows it has catching up to do.

What's interesting in context: Benedict Evans notes that Google has joined Anthropic and OpenAI with a desktop Mac app. The shift from web to local apps is "partly driven by the scope to interact with local apps and files — using the operating system as an actual operating system." For Apple, this means its hardware and OS integration could be a genuine AI advantage — if the Ternus-era company can ship fast enough.

§ 03

Craft

Design Isn't Dying. It's Shifting Left.

The headline from Sidebar / UX Collective this week was from Microsoft Design: when the model is the new medium, shaping how it behaves in humanistic ways is design. The core argument: design isn't being replaced by AI — it's moving earlier in the development process.

Traditionally in software engineering, "shifting left" means addressing issues before they become expensive. For design, this means engaging with technological constraints and real data as early as possible. The Microsoft piece argues: "Design decisions aren't just happening on top of the product — they're happening inside of it."

Design was never about pixels. It's always been about people. Microsoft Design

Sidebar's other lead link, "If You Thought the Speed of Writing Code Was Your Problem, You Have a Problem," argues that AI coding tools are optimizing the wrong thing. Speed of production isn't the bottleneck; judgment and problem definition are.

Memorisely announced a Claude Design workshop — Anthropic's new tool that takes you from design system to high-fidelity prototype in one workflow. Structure your tokens and components, and Claude generates testable product features without switching tools. It's the practical manifestation of the "shifting left" thesis: design systems as inputs, working prototypes as outputs.

The companion Sidebar link, "The AI Adoption Spiral," captures the emotional journey of working with these tools in a humor-tinged diagram. Worth bookmarking for the next time a colleague says "I tried ChatGPT once, it was bad."

§ 04

Branding

Stolen Sweets: Crisis as Brand Play

On March 26, a truck carrying 413,793 limited-edition F1-shaped KitKat bars was intercepted between an Italian factory and its Polish destination. Twelve metric tons of chocolate, gone. Vendry's Case Studied dissected what happened next — and why it worked.

KitKat's public response arrived three days later with the line: "We've always encouraged people to have a break with KitKat — but it seems thieves have taken the message too literally." They appreciated the criminals' "exceptional taste." Memes spread. Ryanair and Domino's jumped in. Then on April Fools' Day, KitKat launched a "Stolen KitKat Tracker" — an online tool where customers enter batch codes to check if their bar was part of the stolen shipment.

Case Studied's takeaways are worth internalizing: stay on-brand even in crisis management (their statement sounded like KitKat, not like a legal team); humor only works when stakes are low (nobody was hurt, supply wasn't affected); and have a crisis playbook ready before you need it. The speed of KitKat's response suggests pre-existing processes — or, as some skeptics note, pre-existing planning.

Whether the heist was real or staged barely matters for the branding lesson: the best crisis responses lean into brand voice, not away from it. KFC did this in 2018 with its "FCK" apology after running out of chicken. KitKat's playbook rhymes.

§ 05

Decision-Making

Predicting Is Hard. Deciding Is the Job.

The OP Letter delivered a deceptively simple essay this week, using the post-mortem of Watches & Wonders 2026 predictions as a lens for executive decision-making. Nine sources predicted a Rolex Milgauss return. Seven predicted a Coke GMT. The OP Letter's own contrarian call: a moonphase on the 1908.

Scorecard: Milgauss — no. Coke GMT — no. Explorer II — no. Moonphase — no. Nobody called the Yacht-Master II return. The pros had patents, dealer relationships, forum sources, CEO interview access. They still called it mostly wrong.

The most common failure mode at senior levels isn't bad decision-making. It's deferred decision-making. Darren, OP Letter

The watch parallel maps perfectly to portfolio-company decision-making: will this GTM motion work? Will agentic AI stall or compound? Will this senior hire stick? The answer is always "you cannot know." Colin Powell's rule: decide between 40% and 70% of the information. Below 40% is gambling. Above 70% is late. Bezos's distinction between Type 1 (irreversible) and Type 2 (reversible) decisions completes the framework.

"I'll take a 60% call made now over a 95% call made six months from now, every time."

Culture & Power

Papal Bull

Today in Tabs handed the column to Liz Lopatto of The Verge, who delivered a devastating and genuinely hilarious account of the Trump administration's escalating feud with Pope Leo XIV. The headline quote: "Pope Leo is WEAK on Crime." The pope's offense? Calling for peace in general terms.

Lopatto traces the entire pipeline: Opus Dei's cultivation of Washington power through "apostolic friendship," Leonard Leo's role on the Opus Dei board while engineering three Supreme Court nominations, the wave of conservative Catholic conversions as career networking, and now the spectacle of JD Vance — a Thiel protégé — trying to Augustine-splain at the pope while Leo was literally in Hippo, the city of Augustine.

It remains to be seen if we're going to get a second, dumber Reformation. Today in Tabs

Meanwhile, the Vatican's main AI advisor published an essay asking whether Peter Thiel should be burned at the stake for his heretical views on the Antichrist. The pope has met with Opus author Gareth Gore, suggesting he may order a full investigation into Opus Dei's American wing. And Trump administration officials have invoked the Avignon papacy — a series of popes installed by France that led to a church schism.

Lopatto's kicker lands perfectly: Pope Leo is promoting Catholic-Islamic friendship and his advisors appear to hate Silicon Valley. "Apparently this is part of the plot of Dune, so we may also have the Butlerian jihad to look forward to."

Outside the Bubble

World & Markets: What's Happening Out There

Tech newsletters live inside their own reality. A quick scan of Google News this morning reframes the week — energy, geopolitics, and a few tech stories the trade press missed.

Strait of Hormuz & the energy shock

Iran has seized two container ships attempting to leave the Gulf as a U.S. blockade of the Strait of Hormuz continues. Thousands more U.S. forces are deploying to the Middle East. The Financial Times reports energy traders are "digging in for a long Iran war" — pricing in prolonged regional instability rather than a quick de-escalation. (Reuters, NYT, NPR, CBS, FT)

Europe's €90B Ukraine loan unlocks — via a pipeline

The Druzhba oil pipeline restart let Hungary drop its veto, unblocking a €90 billion EU assistance package for Ukraine. In the same breath, Russia announced it will suspend Kazakh oil flowing through that same pipeline to Germany. Europe is now examining supply-chain controls on jet fuel as Iran-conflict shortages emerge. (BBC, CNN, DW, Politico, Al Jazeera)
